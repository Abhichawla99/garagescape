Why Calgary Is Hard on Garage Floors

Most garage floor advice online is written for climates that stay frozen all winter or never freeze at all. Calgary is neither, and that's exactly the problem.

A chinook can lift the city from minus twenty to plus ten in a day. For your garage slab, every one of those swings is a damage cycle: snow and slush melt off the car, salt-laden water soaks into the pore structure of the concrete, the temperature drops again, and the water freezes and expands by roughly nine percent. Each expansion pops microscopic flakes off the surface. Do that dozens of times a winter, year after year, and you get the classic Calgary garage floor — pitted, flaking, dusty, with exposed aggregate where the surface paste used to be.

A steadily cold city gets a handful of freeze-thaw cycles a winter. Calgary gets dozens. That's why a fifteen-year-old Calgary slab often looks worse than a forty-year-old one from somewhere the temperature just goes down in November and stays there.

The part road salt plays

Salt makes freeze-thaw worse in two ways. It lowers the freezing point, so water stays liquid longer and soaks deeper into the slab before it finally freezes — deeper water means deeper damage. And the chloride itself chemically attacks the concrete surface and corrodes any rebar close to it. A salty slab doesn't just look bad; it's actively being consumed. Flushing the chloride out every spring is the single most protective thing you can do for a Calgary garage floor.

The Salt Problem, Specifically

Calgary treats winter roads with a salt and gravel mix, and every drive home relocates a little of it onto your garage floor. By March the slab under a daily driver carries a visible white film, a beach of sand and gravel, and a slush line where the car drips. Three things about that load are worth knowing:

  • The white film is chloride, and sweeping doesn't remove it. Salt dissolves into the surface. It has to be flushed with hot water and neutralised — a broom just redistributes it.
  • The grit is abrasive. Sand and gravel underfoot and under tires grinds the surface paste off the slab all winter. It's also why we sweep and vacuum before any water touches the floor — wet grit under a pressure washer scars concrete.
  • The salt attacks what you store, too. Bike drivetrains, tools on the floor, the bottom of metal shelving — anything metal sitting in the salt splash zone corrodes at a rate that surprises people. Getting storage off the floor is half the fix; getting the salt off the floor is the other half.

Oil Stains: Why Pressure Alone Fails

The most common disappointment in garage floor cleaning: someone rents a pressure washer, blasts the oil stain, it looks fixed, and ten days later it's back. That's not bad luck — it's physics. Oil migrates down into the pore network of the slab, sometimes millimetres deep. Pressure cleans the top; the rest wicks back up.

The fix is a poultice — an absorbent compound mixed with solvent, spread over the stain, and left for hours. The solvent re-dissolves the oil, and as the poultice dries, capillary action reverses and pulls the oil up into the absorbent, which is then scraped off and disposed of. Honest limits: a stain that's been feeding into a porous slab for a decade may lighten 70–90% rather than vanish, and some need two cycles. We tell you which you're looking at when we quote.

Cleaning First, Then Sealing

Here's where Calgary differs from the coast in your favour: this is a good climate to seal a garage floor in. Dry summers, low ground moisture, slabs that dry fully — the conditions coastal installers wish they had.

A penetrating siloxane or silane sealer reduces how much salt-laden water the slab can absorb, which directly slows the freeze-thaw cycle that's eating your floor. The order matters though:

  1. Clean and degrease — sealer won't bond through oil and grime
  2. Neutralise the chloride — sealing over salt locks the problem inside the slab
  3. Let the slab dry fully — easy in a Calgary summer
  4. Then seal — and re-flush the floor each spring after

Our clean leaves the floor genuinely ready to seal. If you're considering the bigger step — epoxy or polyaspartic coating — the same rule applies with more zeros on it: coating over a salty, oily or damp slab is how a $4,000 floor fails in two winters. Clean first, test first.

Our Six-Step Calgary Floor Process

1

Slab assessment

Age, spalling, pitting, crack pattern, existing coatings, and how much surface paste is left. This decides pressure, nozzle and chemistry — and whether high pressure is safe on your concrete at all.

2

Dry removal of grit

The winter's sand and gravel is swept and vacuumed off before any water touches the floor. Wet grit under a pressure washer acts like sandblasting media on your slab.

3

Targeted pre-treatment

Degreaser on oil with proper dwell time, oxalic on rust, alkaline on tire film. Set-in oil stains get a poultice applied now so it draws while we do everything else.

4

Hot-water wash and chloride flush

Hot water does what cold can't on salt and grease. A rotating surface cleaner gives even coverage without wand stripes, and the full-slab flush lifts dissolved chloride out of the surface.

5

Neutralise and extract

Salt residue neutralised, wash water extracted rather than pushed out the door — in winter, water flushed onto your driveway becomes a skating rink by evening.

6

Dry-down and sealing report

Forced drying, then a short written note: slab condition, spalling extent, and whether the floor is a good sealing or coating candidate. We don't sell coatings, so the answer is just the answer.

When to Book It

Spring, right after the melt, is the highest-value window. The full winter's salt load is on the slab and removing it stops the chloride damage for the year. Late April into May books out first. Fall is second-best — going into winter with a clean, sealed floor means the season's salt sits on sealer instead of bare concrete. Summer is sealing season, when the slab is warm and bone dry. And mid-winter cleans are genuinely worth it for heavy-use attached garages, because the salt is doing its damage in January, not waiting politely for spring.

One more Calgary-specific reason to keep the floor clean enough to park on: hail season. June through September, the car that lives in the garage doesn't get the dents. A garage too full or too filthy to park in has a very measurable cost here — ask anyone who's been through a June hailstorm with the car on the driveway. If the floor isn't the only problem, our decluttering and full garage cleaning services get the whole space back.

Garage Floor Cleaning Calgary — FAQs

How much does garage floor cleaning cost in Calgary?

A single-car floor starts at $179 and a standard double at $249. Heavy oil staining needing poultice extraction adds $60–$150, and a full salt-neutralise-and-seal-prep package for a double runs $349–$449. We quote on the actual floor, not over the phone.

What's the white residue all over my floor?

Tracked-in road de-icer. Calgary salts and gravels winter roads, your tires carry it in, the slush melts, and evaporation leaves chloride salts as a white film. It isn't cosmetic — chloride accelerates surface deterioration and corrodes anything metal near it. It has to be flushed and neutralised; sweeping just moves it around.

Why is my floor pitting and flaking?

Freeze-thaw spalling, accelerated by chinooks. Water in the slab's pores freezes, expands about nine percent, and pops flakes off the surface. Calgary's chinook cycles run that process dozens of times a winter — far more than steadily cold cities — and salt lets the water soak deeper before freezing. Cleaning removes the salt that feeds the cycle; sealing slows it down.

Can old oil stains actually be removed?

Usually. Not with pressure alone — oil sits deep in the pores and wicks back — but a poultice that re-dissolves the oil and draws it up out of the concrete as it dries genuinely removes it. Decade-old stains on porous slabs may lighten 70–90% rather than vanish, and we tell you which outcome you're looking at before you pay.

Should I seal the floor afterwards?

In Calgary, usually yes — this is a much better sealing climate than the coast. A penetrating sealer reduces how much salt-laden water the slab absorbs, which directly slows freeze-thaw damage. Order matters: clean, neutralise, dry fully, then seal. Sealing over salt locks the problem in.

Do you work in winter?

Yes, for attached and heated garages — most of Calgary. A January clean is worthwhile when salt build-up is heavy, because the chloride damages the slab all winter. Unheated detached garages get scheduled around cold snaps; washing concrete at minus twenty makes an ice rink, not a clean floor.
